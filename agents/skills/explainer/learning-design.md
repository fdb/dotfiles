# Learning design: choices, examples, and evidence

Consult this reference when deciding how to introduce a difficult idea, design practice, or repair a page that assumes too much. These are adaptations for a short visual artifact, not a claim that the artifact implements a complete educational method.

## What to borrow, and where to be careful

### Montessori: a prepared environment and visible consequences

Make the first activity approachable, its materials orderly, and the consequence of an action inspectable. Isolate one difficult relationship before combining variables. Let the reader repeat and correct an attempt without penalty. For example, show where quantities fail to balance rather than only announcing that an answer is wrong.

AMI describes concrete materials that embody abstractions and ways learners can detect and correct errors. These suggest interface design choices; a mouse-driven diagram does not reproduce physical materials, sensory experience, or a teacher's observation. Adults also benefit from explicit explanations and may prefer to move directly to symbols.

Sources: AMI, [Sensorial Materials Promoting Learning](https://montessori-ami.org/node/6823) and [Control of Error](https://montessori-ami.org/trainingvoices/control-of-error).

### MIT Teaching + Learning Lab: start with usable capabilities

Choose what the reader should be able to do, then choose evidence of that capability and the preparation it requires. Match the check to the goal: recognizing a term does not show that someone can explain a mechanism. Activate relevant prior knowledge and repair missing or misleading connections before depending on them.

This need not look like a syllabus. A short opening case can both establish relevance and bring familiar knowledge into use.

Sources: MIT T+LL, [Backward Design](https://tll.mit.edu/teaching-resources/course-design/backward-design/) and [Retain, Organize and Integrate Knowledge](https://tll.mit.edu/teaching-resources/how-to-teach/help-students-retain-organize-and-integrate-knowledge/).

### Worked examples: support novices, then let them take over

Show reasoning through an example when unfamiliar elements would make unguided search overwhelming. Follow with a partially supported attempt and then a different case. Offer the example as help for experienced readers instead of making them repeat it.

T+LL reviews worked-example research and the importance of prior expertise. Use that to choose the amount of guidance; do not equate learning with either maximum explanation or maximum discovery.

Source: MIT T+LL, [Worked Examples](https://tll.mit.edu/teaching-resources/how-people-learn/worked-examples/).

### Lifelong Kindergarten: make, vary, reflect

Give the reader a small thing to build or modify, a meaningful choice of direction, and room to experiment. A useful activity can have several successful outcomes. In a longer piece, return to a creation after learning another idea and invite revision.

Resnick's creative-learning approach emphasizes projects, passion, peers, and play, with cycles of imagining, creating, playing, sharing, and reflecting. For a standalone page, a copyable configuration or prompt to explain a creation to someone can support exchange. It does not supply a peer community. Do not add accounts, public posting, or a fake social layer to satisfy the four Ps.

Sources: MIT Lifelong Kindergarten, [Learning Creative Learning](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/) and Resnick, [Lifelong Kindergarten, chapter excerpt](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/chapter1-excerpt.pdf).

### Retrieval, explanation, and transfer: test use, not familiarity

Ask readers to recall a relationship or justify a prediction before consulting an answer. Provide corrective explanation. Later, change the context so they must identify what carries over. Let them compare their reasoning with a worked response rather than ask only “does that make sense?”

The IES practice guide recommends worked examples with practice, linked concrete and abstract representations, explanatory questions, retrieval, and spacing. Its evidence ratings differ: pre-questions have weaker support than quizzes that revisit content. Do not make a prediction prompt compulsory before every new idea or describe every recommendation as equally established.

A question revisited later in the page provides another encounter, but does not establish long-term retention. Offer a take-away challenge for another day where useful. A fluent reading experience and an immediate correct response are limited evidence of learning.

Sources: IES, [Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/1); MIT T+LL, [Metacognition](https://tll.mit.edu/teaching-resources/how-people-learn/metacognition/).

## Example: queues for a curious beginner

**Goal:** explain why equal average arrival and service rates can still produce waiting, and compare ways to reduce it. Do not start with a utilization formula or a queue-length plot.

- Establish a single job arriving, waiting if needed, receiving service, and leaving. Show what one time unit means. Use a short, labelled synthetic schedule.
- Work through a few arrivals on a timeline. Keep a list of waiting jobs beside it. Explain the mapping before introducing a chart of queue length.
- Keep service duration fixed and let the reader place one arrival. Show the resulting start and finish times; let them correct their prediction.
- Compare evenly spaced arrivals with a burst using the same job count and total interval. Ask why equal averages do not imply equal waiting.
- Let the reader construct another schedule, then introduce service variability or a second server. Explain which earlier relationships still apply.
- Give a fresh schedule and ask which proposed change would reduce waiting and why. Supply a worked comparison and the model's limits. Do not imply that this finite example establishes a universal stability result.

The page develops event order, waiting, rates, variability, and intervention. It does not need a dramatic threshold. Its visual language can come from the timetable and the jobs being handled, rather than generic dots and sliders.

## Example: historical explanation without a simulation

**Goal:** compare two accounts of an event and distinguish what each source supports from what remains uncertain.

Establish the event, chronology, actors, and provenance before presenting excerpts. Model one inference from an annotated passage, including its limits. Give another short passage and invite the reader to identify evidence for a claim; show an explanation they can compare with their own. Put conflicting accounts side by side and ask what further evidence would help. Use verified, appropriately quoted sources.

Here the useful interaction might be linked annotation or an optional response reveal. There is no need to turn history into a deterministic model or manufacture an inevitable verdict.

## Common repairs

| Failure | Repair |
|---|---|
| The opening assumes specialist vocabulary | Replace the first abstraction with a concrete case; teach the needed terms there |
| The reader can operate the toy but cannot explain it | Add a worked trace and a prediction with feedback; make the representation mapping explicit |
| Every section is another disconnected demo | Carry an example forward and require earlier ideas to interpret the next one |
| The lesson works only for the shown example | Add a contrasting case and ask what changed and what stayed invariant |
| An open sandbox leaves the reader lost | Supply a useful starting state, a small goal, and optional help |
| Everything is over-guided | Offer direct access to a challenge and optional refreshers |
| The page feels bland | Find a specific object, genuine question, or consequential case; vary composition to match the content |
| Review says “clear” but tests nothing | Ask for an explanation or a new-case attempt and inspect where reasoning fails |

Do not label readers by supposed fixed visual, auditory, or kinesthetic learning styles. Select representations for the concept, accessibility needs, and task. Educational traditions offer useful ideas; this particular skill still needs evaluation through real use.
